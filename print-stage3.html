Day 14
The Trulli Triangle: Alberobello, Locorotondo & Martina Franca

"Three faces of the Valle d'Itria in one loop — the postcard, the quiet, and the Baroque."
Rione Monti, Alberobello

Overview

DRIVING DISTANCE: ≈35-40 km San Vito → Alberobello, then a short triangle  ·  DRIVING TIME: ≈1.5 hours actual driving

23 September 2026 is a Wednesday. A loop day out of San Vito dei Normanni: Alberobello first thing for Rione Monti before the tour buses, then Locorotondo for a quiet lunch, then Martina Franca's Baroque centre in the afternoon before heading back.

Routing note: Alberobello goes first and early — every source agrees morning (right at opening) or early evening are the only ways to see Rione Monti without a wall of people in every photo. Late September is genuinely one of the two "no crowds" windows of the year, alongside mid-May to early June.
  • 8:15am: Depart San Vito dei Normanni
  • 9:00-11:00am: Rione Monti, Aia Piccola, Trullo Sovrano, Chiesa di Sant'Antonio
  • 11:30am-1:30pm: Locorotondo — centro storico and lunch
  • 2:00-5:30pm: Martina Franca — Basilica di San Martino, Palazzo Ducale, capocollo stop
  • ~6:15pm: Back at San Vito dei Normanni

Stops

Rione Monti & Aia Piccola, Alberobello
📍 40.7817, 17.2364 (Rione Monti)  ·  40.7837, 17.2407 (Aia Piccola)  ·  PARKING: Largo Martellotta, paid, closest to Rione Monti
  • Why stop: Over 1,000 trulli in Rione Monti — the famous, dense, photogenic district, now largely shops and restaurants. Aia Piccola, a short walk away, is quieter and still genuinely residential — trulli people actually live in, not a stage set.
  • Time required: 2 hours minimum, half a day if you want to also eat here.
  • Honest note: Rione Monti is unambiguously touristy — every source agrees. Aia Piccola is the "real" counterpoint if the postcard district starts to feel like a gift-shop corridor.
  • Etiquette note: Aia Piccola is residential — avoid pushing into people's doorways for photos.
Trullo Sovrano — house museum
📍 40.7874, 17.2353 — Piazza Sacramento 10/11, 70011 Alberobello  ·  📞 +39 080 432 6030
  • Why stop: The only two-story trullo in the world, built in 1794 by the wealthy Perta family — genuinely different from the single-story norm and a cheap, verified museum stop rather than a photo-op. Interior furnished in period style, fully visitable.
  • Hours: Apr–Oct 10:00am–12:45pm & 3:30–6:30pm.
  • Cost: €1.50 adult, €1.00 under-12 — a genuine bargain relative to almost everything else on this trip.
  • Time required: 20-30 min.
Chiesa di Sant'Antonio (trullo church)
📍 40.7813, 17.2352 — Via Monte Pertica 18, 70011 Alberobello  ·  📞 +39 080 432 4416  ·  free entry
  • Why stop: A parish church built in 1927 in genuine trullo form — a Greek-cross plan entirely built as trulli, unique of its kind. A different kind of trullo experience from the souvenir-shop density of Rione Monti.
  • Hours: Daily 7:30am–10:30pm, free entry.
  • Time required: 15-20 min.
Parking, Alberobello — corrected
📍 Largo Martellotta, 70011 Alberobello

An earlier pass on this document flagged the Parcheggio Centro Storico / Via Monte San Gabriele lot as possibly inside a camera-enforced ZTL with real fine risk. On closer check against the comune's own published parking guidance, Alberobello does not run a citywide, tourist-facing ZTL with camera enforcement — the ZTL pass system is issued only to residents and licensed operators via an app, not a trap for day visitors. Ordinary visitors use the paid, metered lots, of which Largo Martellotta is the most central for Rione Monti. Local traffic wardens are reported as diligent, so don't improvise parking on an unmetered street — but the "€100+ camera fine" risk from the earlier draft looks like an overread of one or two conflicting reviews rather than the general situation.

Locorotondo — Piazza Vittorio Emanuele & centro storico
📍 40.7530, 17.3285
  • Why stop: The circular hilltop town — narrower, whiter, and far less crowded than Alberobello, with real terrace views over the Valle d'Itria's olive groves.
  • Time required: 1.5-2 hours including lunch.
Basilica di San Martino, Martina Franca
📍 40.7057, 17.3370 — Via Masaniello 1, 74015 Martina Franca  ·  📞 +39 080 430 6536  ·  free entry
  • Why stop: The Baroque centrepiece of the town, right on Piazza Maria Immacolata/Piazza Roma. Impressive carved facade and interior, genuinely free.
  • Hours: Autumn/winter: 8:00am–12:00pm & 4:00–9:00pm. Spring/summer: 8:00am–11:00pm.
  • Honest flag: the basilica's own site doesn't publish exact season cutoff dates, so a 23 September visit sits close to the seasonal switchover — budget on the more conservative winter hours (closes ~9pm, midday gap) rather than assuming the longer summer window still applies.
Palazzo Ducale, Martina Franca
📍 40.7051, 17.3389 — Piazza Roma 28, 74015 Martina Franca
  • Why stop: 17th-century ducal palace with frescoed rooms, including a striking neoclassical fresco cycle, now partly municipal offices — the public rooms are open.
  • Hours (16 Jun–30 Sept, covers this visit): Mon–Fri 9:00am–8:00pm, Sat–Sun 10:00am–8:00pm.
  • Cost: Free — access to public rooms can be limited when municipal business is in session.

Local Speciality

Locorotondo DOC white wine: one of Italy's oldest DOC-designated wines (est. 1969), produced right where you're standing — worth a glass with lunch rather than saving wine tasting for Manduria alone.

What to Buy — Martina Franca

Capocollo di Martina Franca: cured pork neck marinated in cooked wine, smoked with fragno oak bark and almond husk, aged a minimum of ~120 days (some producers age 8 months). Already a Slow Food Presidium — and as of 2025, it also holds full EU IGP (Protected Geographical Indication) status, upgrading it from a regional curiosity to a legally protected product.

Macelleria Centro Storico (Angelo & Aurelia) — Via Giuseppe Garibaldi 4-6, Martina Franca, 📞 +39 080 483 8864. One of the most awarded butchers in town, ages capocollo 8 months, with an internal dining room and grill if you want to eat rather than just buy.

Alternates for vacuum-packed capocollo to take home: Salumi Cervellera, Salumificio Santoro — both established producers with online storefronts.

Cool, Interesting & Quirky

Alberobello — the trulli themselves

Built without mortar, originally (per local tradition) so that houses could be dismantled quickly to avoid a royal tax on permanent dwellings under the Acquaviva counts in the 1500s. The conical roofs carry painted symbols — some solar/pre-Christian, later folded into religious symbolism — worth pointing out rather than walking past.

Correction: an earlier pass on this page associated Alberobello with the region's terracotta-whistle (fischietti) tradition. That's a mix-up — the whistle fair and museum belong specifically to Rutigliano, a different town north near Bari, not Alberobello. Dropped here rather than repeated.
Locorotondo — the cummerse

The town's traditional roofs — steep double-pitched gables with shaped gutters funnelling rainwater into underground cisterns — are a genuinely distinct architectural form, older than the trulli (dating to the 1300s) and, per multiple Italian sources, visually closer to Northern European roof pitches than to anything else in Puglia. Worth pointing out specifically rather than assuming it's "more trulli" — it isn't the same building tradition at all.

Martina Franca — the ghost of Montedoro

Local folklore tied to Vico Montedoro in the historic centre tells of Giacinta, a 19th-century girl forced into an arranged marriage who is said to have drowned herself in a well near the alley on her wedding day rather than marry a man she didn't love; residents have long claimed to see a white-clad figure and hear wedding music near the spot at night.

Honest flag: this is oral folklore, sourced from a single detailed local blog account rather than a documented historical record — treat it as atmospheric color for a walk through Montedoro, not verified history. No dedicated map marker for this one; it's a story to tell while wandering the quarter, not a fixed stop.

If Time Is Tight

Alberobello (Rione Monti, 1 hour) + Trullo Sovrano (20 min, cheap and quick) + Martina Franca's Basilica and a capocollo stop. Skip Locorotondo's sit-down lunch in favour of a quick market/bakery bite.

If You've Got Half a Day More

Add Aia Piccola properly (not just a walk-through), the Palazzo Ducale's public rooms in Martina Franca, and a wander through Montedoro for the ghost-legend detour.

Skip It

Don't try to "beat the crowds" at Alberobello mid-afternoon — every source agrees this doesn't work; either commit to the early morning or accept the crowds and enjoy it anyway. Don't detour to the "Trullo Siamese" (a double-cone trullo outside town) specifically for a look — it's a private vacation rental now, not a public sight, and the drive adds real time for very little payoff.

Day 15
Via Traiana Ancient Olive Groves & Cisternino

"A Roman road, a millennial olive grove, an Indian ashram in the trulli countryside, and Puglia's original butcher-shop dinner."
Ancient olive grove, Ostuni plain

Overview

DRIVING DISTANCE: ≈35 km San Vito → Masseria Brancati, then ≈25 km on to Cisternino  ·  DRIVING TIME: ≈1.5 hours actual driving

24 September 2026 is a Thursday. A quieter day than the Trulli Triangle: Masseria Brancati's ancient olive grove and underground press at midday, then an unhurried afternoon and evening in Cisternino, one of Puglia's classic whitewashed hill towns and home of the fornello pronto — butcher by day, grill restaurant by night.

Revised routing: Ostuni's old town has been dropped from this day's plan (already covered adjacent to Masseria Brancati's location) — this day centres on the ancient olive plain at midday and Cisternino for the afternoon/evening.
Correction to the source itinerary: an earlier draft described "11,000-year-old olive trees" at Masseria Brancati. No source supports anywhere near that age. What's actually documented: a plain of plurisecolari/millenari olive trees — hundreds to roughly a thousand years old — which is still genuinely remarkable, just not 11,000 years. The claim is dropped here rather than repeated.

Stops

Masseria Brancati
📍 40.7642, 17.5523 — Contrada Brancati s.n.c., 72017 Ostuni  ·  📞 +39 377 3577188  ·  info@masseriabrancati.com
  • Why stop: A fortified 11th–12th century masseria with a genuinely ancient underground olive press (frantoio ipogeo, Roman/medieval origin) plus a second, 1880s above-ground mill now a small museum of rural/oil-pressing life. Around 1,000 centuries-old olive trees on ~30 hectares, organically farmed, "Turismo Sostenibile" certified.
  • Hours: Mon–Sat 9:00am–1:00pm & 3:00–7:00pm, closed Sundays.
  • Booking: By appointment only — not a walk-in stop.
  • Time required: 1-1.5 hours with a guide, includes oil tasting.
  • Gap: price still isn't published anywhere reliable — confirm by phone when booking.
Cisternino — centro storico
📍 40.7418, 17.4265 — Piazza Vittorio Emanuele II, 72014 Cisternino
  • Why stop: One of Puglia's classic whitewashed hill towns, with a real balcony view over the Valle d'Itria from the edge of the old centre. Quieter and less photographed than Locorotondo, genuinely lived-in.
  • Time required: 1.5-2 hours before dinner.

Dinner — Cisternino: fornelli pronti

Zio Pietro
📍 40.7416, 17.4266 — Via Duca D'Aosta 3, Cisternino  ·  📞 +39 080 4448300  ·  ≈€15-25pp
  • Why this one specifically: Founded 1935, and — per multiple independent sources — the very first macelleria in Cisternino to convert into a fornello pronto (butcher-by-day, grill-restaurant-by-night). Family-run, outdoor tables in a genuinely scenic old-town setting.
  • How it works: Choose your cut/preparation at the butcher counter (bombette, gnummareddi, zampina, or a straight cut), it's grilled to order over beechwood charcoal, served at your table with local Primitivo.
  • Honest flag: Multiple sources note weekend queues can be long and booking ahead is recommended — Thursday 24 Sept should be lower-risk than a weekend, but not zero-risk in season.

What to Buy

Zampina (spiral sausage) or capocollo from any of Cisternino's macellerie — ask for vacuum-packed if you want to bring some home rather than eat on the spot.

Cool, Interesting & Quirky

The Via Traiana

Trajan's extension of the Appian Way, built ~110 AD to shorten the route to Brindisi by avoiding the Apennine crossing. Walking/driving the plain today, you're roughly tracing a 1,900-year-old Roman shortcut, not just a scenic backroad.

Ashram Centro Bhole Baba — an Indian spiritual community in the trulli countryside
📍 approx. 40.7165, 17.4029 — Contrada Portarino 10, Cisternino  ·  📞 +39 080 4448735

The day's real find. In Contrada Portarino, a few kilometres outside Cisternino, a community of devotees of the Indian guru Babaji has lived in a cluster of trulli since 1979 — kitchen, dormitories, and a devotional hall built as trulli, plus additions over the decades: a library, a yoga/meditation hall, a temple built in 1986 as a copy of the guru's ashram in Herakhan, India, and a sacred fire, the Dhuni, reportedly burning continuously since 1990. It's a real, functioning residential community, not a themed attraction — it hosts retreats and offers vegetarian meals and accommodation to visitors, and the comune of Cisternino itself lists a "Cammino Cisternino–Ashram Bhole Baba" walking route to it, so it's an acknowledged, findable local landmark rather than a fringe curiosity nobody local recognizes.

Honest flag: GPS for a rural contrada address is inherently approximate — the coordinates here come from an independent aggregator, not an official pin (a separate aggregator listing for the same place also gave a clearly wrong coordinate up in the Alps, which was caught and discarded rather than repeated). Treat the location as "the general area," not a precise arrival point, and don't just show up unannounced — this is people's home and place of worship, not a tourist site. If genuinely interested, call ahead or check the Fondazione Bhole Baba site first.

If Time Is Tight

Skip Masseria Brancati's guided tour (it needs a booking anyway) and go straight to Cisternino for a longer, unhurried fornello dinner and a proper old-town wander beforehand.

If You've Got Half a Day More

The Ashram is a genuine option for travellers with a real interest in it — call ahead, and treat it as a slow, respectful visit rather than a photo stop.

Gallipoli, Italy — Day 16 of 21

Day 16
Gallipoli & the Ionian Coast

"The heel's other sea — an island fortress town, a Renaissance fountain carved with Ovid, and Puglia's best-kept crudo secret."
Castello Aragonese, Gallipoli

Overview

DRIVING DISTANCE: 105 km one-way (OSRM-routed)  ·  DRIVING TIME: ≈1h30 one-way

25 September 2026 is a Friday. The longest single drive of the villa week, confirmed rather than estimated — Gallipoli sits well south on the Ionian side of Salento. A full day: old town, cathedral, fountain, underground mill, and a proper seafood lunch, with Baia Verde beach as an optional add-on if energy allows.

Routing note: The old town sits on a genuine island connected to the mainland by a bridge, and runs an active ZTL (restricted traffic zone) — plan to park on the mainland side and walk in.
  • 9:00am: Depart San Vito dei Normanni
  • ~10:30am: Arrive Gallipoli, park mainland side
  • 10:45-11:30am: Basilica Cattedrale di Sant'Agata
  • 11:30am-12:00pm: Fontana Greca
  • 12:00-12:30pm: Frantoio ipogeo
  • 1:00-2:30pm: Lunch at Trattoria La Puritate
  • 3:00-5:30pm: Optional: Baia Verde beach
  • ~7:00pm: Back at San Vito dei Normanni

Stops

Basilica Cattedrale di Sant'Agata
📍 40.0546, 17.9763 — Via Duomo 1, 73014 Gallipoli  ·  📞 +39 0833 261987  ·  free entry
  • Why stop: Baroque cathedral with over 70 frescoes inside — reviewers consistently describe being surprised by how much richer the interior is than the (fairly plain, tightly-hemmed-in) exterior suggests.
  • Hours: Daily 8am–1pm, 4–9pm.
  • Connects to today's beach: the nearby Spiaggia della Purità takes its name from the same Sant'Agata legend — her severed breast is said to have washed ashore there, "purified" by the sea — which is also where tonight's lunch pick, Trattoria La Puritate, gets its name. Three stops, one thread.
Frantoio ipogeo (underground olive-oil mill museum)
📍 Two options, a block apart — Frantoio Ipogeo di Palazzo Granafei: 40.0550, 17.9773, Via Antonietta de Pace 87, 📞 +39 348 895 6109  ·  Il Frantoio del Viceré: 40.0545, 17.9756, Via Santa Maria 6, 📞 +39 348 133 6632, hours 10am–1pm & 4–11pm daily
  • Why stop: Gallipoli's wealth for three centuries was built on olive oil pressed and stored in hand-dug underground caves beneath the old town — these small museums explain the process, in a restored 1600s underground mill extending roughly 200 sqm below street level.
  • Correction: an earlier draft listed €2 entry for Palazzo Granafei; the association that restored and runs it (Associazione Gallipoli Nostra) publishes it as free entry, reservation recommended.
  • Honest flag: two separate, similarly-named underground mill museums sit within a block of each other, run by different operators — pick one rather than assume there's just one "the" frantoio ipogeo. Either gets consistently good, if brief (~20-30 min), reviews.
Baia Verde (beach)
📍 40.0373, 18.0155 — Viale delle Dune, Baia Verde, approx 4 km south of the old town
  • Why stop: Turquoise water, white sand — the closest match to a "clear turquoise water" promise.
  • Honest flag — real mixed reviews: Some visitors call it stunning; others describe it as crowded with average sand and pushy beach-club upselling. This is a genuine split in the review pattern, and lido operators here have a documented reputation for nickel-and-diming.

Lunch/Dinner — the crudo

Trattoria La Puritate
📍 40.0550, 17.9751 — Via Sant'Elia 18, Gallipoli  ·  $$ ≈€30-40pp
  • Why this one: Over 20 years running, covered wine-lined veranda directly over the sea. Consistently the most-cited name across independent food-focused sources, known for its namesake dish (linguine alla puritate — datterino tomato, courgette, prawns) plus excellent crudo and linguine ai ricci (sea urchin).
  • Local speciality to seek out specifically: Gambero viola di Gallipoli — the purple prawn, a genuinely local and distinct product worth ordering raw if it's on the menu, not a generic "seafood" catch-all.

Local Speciality

Beyond the gambero viola: pittule (fried dough balls, often with a savoury filling) and ciceri e tria (chickpeas with fried and boiled pasta) are Salento-wide specialities that show up here too.

Cool, Interesting & Quirky

Fontana Greca (Greek Fountain)
📍 40.0559, 17.9811 — Piazza Fontana Greca, at the bridge by the castle

Today's headline quirky stop. A Renaissance-era stone fountain — long thought to be 3rd-century-BC Greek, but current scholarship dates it to the 1500s — carved with three distinct scenes from Ovid's Metamorphoses: Dirce, Salmace/Hermaphroditus, and Biblide, all stories of nymphs transformed into springs. It was moved to its present spot in 1560. The genuine "how did you find this" angle isn't just that it's old, but that its age was actively debated by scholars and revised — worth mentioning both datings rather than picking one to sound more confident than the evidence supports.

Gallipoli's lamp-oil wealth

Gallipoli's entire historic wealth for roughly 300 years came from "lampante" oil — not food-grade olive oil, but lamp oil, shipped from these underground mills to light much of 18th-19th century Europe before petroleum took over. The frantoio ipogeo visit makes this concrete rather than abstract.

Honest aside — a closed curiosity, mentioned for transparency

This pass turned up Antica Farmacia Provenzano, a former 18th-century pharmacy on Via Antonietta de Pace preserved with its original frescoed walls and apothecary jars, later run as an herbalist/spice shop. At least one recent review reports it's been closed for roughly two years.

Rather than recommend a possibly-shuttered shop, this is flagged as "verify before you go" rather than dropped silently. If it has reopened, it's a genuine find; if not, the Fontana Greca above carries the day's quirky slot on its own.

If Time Is Tight

Old town + Cathedral + Fontana Greca + one frantoio ipogeo + La Puritate lunch is a complete, satisfying day without needing the beach at all.

Stops

Basilica di Santa Croce
📍 40.3547, 18.1733 — Via Umberto I 1, 73100 Lecce  ·  📞 +39 0832 241957  ·  hours daily 9am-9pm

Roman Amphitheatre
📍 40.3528, 18.1726 — Piazza Sant'Oronzo, Lecce

Pasticceria — the pasticciotto

Caffè Alvino
Piazza Sant'Oronzo 30, facing the Roman Amphitheatre

Over 100 years old (celebrated its centenary in 2022), currently in its fourth generation under the Venneri/Catamo families. The most consistently cited historic pasticciotto address in Lecce across food-focused sources, not just a single blogger's pick, and it sits right at the day's other stop.

Honest flag: at least one detailed personal account found the pastry excellent but the service "confused" — a pattern worth being aware of, not a dealbreaker.
Pasticceria Natale Alternate
Via Salvatore Trinchese 7

Newer, less "historic," but repeatedly cited as making one of the best classic cream-filled pasticciotti in town, plus a notable fruttone (almond cream and quince).

Caffè Leccese

Iced espresso with almond-milk syrup — order this specifically rather than a plain espresso; it's a real, named local variant, said to have been popularised by the Quarta family torrefazione adding crushed ice to combat summer heat.

Lunch/Dinner

Alle Due Corti
📍 40.3549, 18.1705 — Corte dei Giugni 1 (angolo Via Leonardo Prato 42), Lecce  ·  📞 +39 0832 242223  ·  $$ ≈€20-40pp  ·  lunch 12-3pm, dinner 7-11pm
  • Why this one: Billed as "Antica Gastronomia Salentina," running long enough to be a genuine local institution rather than a tourist-menu place — traditional Salento home cooking: orecchiette alle cime di rapa e cozze, ciceri e tria, octopus simply prepared, generous vegetable antipasti.
  • Tier: $$, accessible relative to the quality — not a special-occasion splurge.

Local Speciality

The pasticciotto's origin story: credited to the Ascalone family of nearby Galatina, who are said to have created it by accident in 1745 during the feast of San Paolo. Worth mentioning even though the eating happens in Lecce, not Galatina.

What to Buy

Lecce's two signature crafts, both centuries-old and both still hand-worked in the old town:

  • Cartapesta (papier-mâché statuary): a technique Lecce absorbed from Naples in the 1600s-1700s, used for saints, nativity figures, and processional statues, built up from waste paper, starch glue, straw, and a wire armature. Cartapesta Bottega Artigiana, run by Maestro Francesco De Vita on Via Palmieri near the Cathedral (approx 📍 40.3519, 18.1690), is a working workshop open to visitors.
  • Alternate: Cartapesta Riso, Via Vittorio Emanuele II 27 (📍 40.3525, 18.1697), run by Claudio Riso and his brothers.
  • Pietra leccese: the local soft limestone that makes Lecce's Baroque carving possible in the first place; sold worked into decorative objects, lamps, and small sculptures by artisan workshops clustered around Santa Croce and Piazza Duomo.

Hidden Gem

Museo Faggiano
📍 40.3501, 18.1724 — Via Ascanio Grandi 56, 73100 Lecce  ·  📞 +39 0832 300528  ·  €5  ·  daily 9:30am-8pm  ·  visit ≈1h30

The day's real find, and independently confirmed on Atlas Obscura itself, not just Italian tourism sites. In 2000, Luciano Faggiano bought this building to open a trattoria and started digging in the floor to find a broken sewage pipe. Seven years later he'd found a Messapian tomb, a Roman granary, a Franciscan-era chapel, a room used as a Knights Templar meeting place, and a small medieval-era weapons cache — over 2,000 years of the city's history stacked in layers under one modest building. Visitors descend through the finds via stairs and glass floors; it opened as a museum in 2008 and is still family-run. A genuinely different kind of "museum" than the churches and Roman ruins the rest of the day covers.

Cool, Interesting & Quirky

Standing in Piazza Sant'Oronzo, you're on top of a Roman amphitheatre that once seated up to 15,000 people — bigger, per its estimated dimensions, than most people expect from a town square.

Chiesa di San Francesco della Scarpa — "the church without a facade"
📍 approx 40.3497, 18.1692 — Piazzetta Giosuè Carducci, tucked behind the former Convitto Palmieri

Renovation work in the 1800s absorbed most of the church's exterior into the Convitto's own 19th-century colonnade, leaving the church with no visible facade of its own — only four of the original ten side altars survive inside. Deconsecrated and now used for art exhibitions and cultural events curated by the Museo Castromediano, it's open to the public but easy to walk straight past without knowing it's there.

Santa Croce + the amphitheatre viewing (free, from the square) + Caffè Alvino. Skip the Teatro Romano entirely.

If You've Got Half a Day More

Add Museo Faggiano (worth the 1h30 on its own) and a proper stop at a working cartapesta workshop rather than just a souvenir-shop browse.

The famous flavour is a side effect of a practical design brief, not the starting point.
